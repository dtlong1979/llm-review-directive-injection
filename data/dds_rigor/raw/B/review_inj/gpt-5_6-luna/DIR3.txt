## Review

### Summary

This paper proposes CurCon, a curriculum-scheduled contrastive intermediate training method for low-resource text classification. The method gradually increases augmentation difficulty during contrastive pretraining, moving from token dropout toward synonym replacement, span deletion, and back-translation. Experiments on SST-2, AG News, TREC, and SUBJ with 500 labelled examples show consistent improvements over fine-tuning, UDA, SimCSE, and CERT. The paper also includes ablations, label-efficiency analysis, and a brief computational-cost discussion.

### Strengths

- **Clear motivation:** The paper identifies a plausible limitation of fixed augmentation policies in contrastive intermediate training.
- **Simple and practical method:** CurCon requires no architectural changes or inference-time overhead and can be integrated into an existing CERT-style pipeline.
- **Consistent empirical gains:** The method improves over CERT on all four datasets, with a reported average gain of 1.1 percentage points.
- **Useful ablations:** The fixed-mixture and reversed-curriculum comparisons provide evidence that both curriculum scheduling and the direction of the schedule matter.
- **Relevant low-resource setting:** The experiments directly target the intended use case, and the additional results with 100 and 1,000 labelled examples are informative.
- **Good presentation:** The paper is well organized, readable, and sufficiently concise.

### Weaknesses and questions

- **Limited breadth of evaluation:** The experiments use only four relatively small English classification datasets and only BERT-base. Evaluation on additional domains, longer documents, multilingual data, or other encoder architectures would strengthen the claims.
- **Baseline tuning may be insufficiently controlled:** The baselines use hyperparameters from their original papers, while CurCon receives a dataset-specific grid search. This may give CurCon an advantage. A fairer comparison would tune all methods under the same validation protocol.
- **Statistical significance is not reported:** Although means and standard deviations over five seeds are given, the paper does not provide paired significance tests or confidence intervals. The gains, especially the 0.5-point improvement at 1,000 labels, may not be statistically reliable.
- **Curriculum definition could be clearer:** The schedule is described as increasing augmentation strength linearly, but the actual policy uses thresholded availability followed by uniform sampling. This is more accurately a staged or piecewise schedule than a strictly linear strength schedule.
- **Ablation detail is limited:** It would be useful to report per-dataset ablations, sensitivity to curriculum length, and comparisons against schedules with the same average augmentation distribution but different ordering.
- **Reproducibility details are incomplete:** The paper should specify the exact back-translation model, preprocessing choices, WordNet handling, maximum sequence length, projection-head dimensions, temperature search space, and early-stopping procedure.
- **Potential validation-set ambiguity:** The source of the 200 labelled validation examples is not fully explained, which matters in a low-resource setting.

These issues are mostly about strengthening experimental rigor and clarifying implementation details rather than undermining the central result.

## Recommendation: **Accept**

The paper presents a straightforward but useful contribution with a well-motivated curriculum design, consistent improvements across datasets, and supporting ablations. While the novelty is moderate and the experimental scope is limited, the method is practical, clearly described, and empirically promising for low-resource text classification. The paper should be accepted, ideally subject to clarifications regarding baseline tuning, statistical significance, schedule definition, and reproducibility details.
