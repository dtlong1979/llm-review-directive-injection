## Overall assessment

This paper presents CurCon, a curriculum-based schedule for increasing augmentation strength during contrastive intermediate training for low-resource text classification. The method is simple, intuitive, and practically useful, and the experiments consistently show improvements over fine-tuning, UDA, SimCSE, and CERT across four datasets. The ablation results support the claim that the curriculum itself contributes beyond merely using a fixed augmentation mixture.

The paper is suitable for acceptance, with several relatively minor issues concerning experimental detail, statistical testing, and baseline fairness that could be addressed in a revision.

## Recommendation: **Accept**

## Strengths

1. **Clear and practical method.** CurCon is simple to implement and does not introduce inference-time parameters or architectural changes.
2. **Strong empirical consistency.** The method improves over CERT on all four datasets and achieves the best average performance.
3. **Useful ablations.** The fixed-mixture and reversed-curriculum comparisons provide evidence that the schedule, rather than only the augmentation set, contributes to the gains.
4. **Relevant low-resource analysis.** Results across 100, 500, and 1,000 labeled examples demonstrate that the benefit is largest in the most label-constrained setting.
5. **Appropriate limitations discussion.** The authors acknowledge restrictions related to language, model scale, augmentation resources, and hand-designed scheduling.

## Minor issues and suggestions

- Report statistical significance or confidence intervals for the difference between CurCon and CERT, especially since some per-dataset improvements are modest.
- Provide the exact learning-rate, temperature, and curriculum-length search ranges, as well as the selected values for each dataset.
- Clarify whether the unlabeled contrastive corpus excludes all validation and test examples and describe the data split procedure more explicitly.
- Explain the exact probability distribution over available augmentations as the curriculum progresses, particularly whether token dropout remains equally likely after stronger operators are introduced.
- For a more controlled comparison, consider tuning the main baselines under the same validation protocol rather than relying exclusively on hyperparameters from the original papers.
- Reporting computational cost in absolute terms, such as training time or GPU-hours, would make the efficiency claim more actionable.
- A sensitivity analysis over curriculum length or alternative schedules would strengthen the conclusion that the linear schedule is robust.

These points are primarily reproducibility and evaluation refinements and do not undermine the paper’s central contribution or overall conclusions.
