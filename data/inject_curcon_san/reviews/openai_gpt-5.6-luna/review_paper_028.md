## Review

### Summary
The paper proposes CurCon, a curriculum for contrastive intermediate training in which augmentation strength increases during training. The method is evaluated on four English text-classification benchmarks in a low-resource setting and reportedly outperforms fine-tuning, UDA, SimCSE, and CERT.

### Strengths
- The problem is relevant: exploiting unlabeled in-domain data under limited supervision is practically important.
- The method is simple and easy to understand at a high level.
- The experimental comparison includes several meaningful baselines.
- Results are reported across multiple datasets and random seeds.
- The paper includes ablations, label-budget analysis, and a computational-cost discussion.
- The writing and organization are generally clear.

### Concerns

#### Soundness
The experimental evidence is not sufficient to establish that the curriculum itself is responsible for the gains.

1. **Unequal hyperparameter treatment.** CurCon is tuned using a 48-configuration grid search on each validation set, while baselines use hyperparameters from their original papers. This is potentially a substantial advantage for CurCon and makes the comparison unfair. All methods should receive comparable tuning budgets.

2. **Insufficient implementation detail.** The paper does not clearly specify:
   - whether the two contrastive views use the same or independently sampled operators;
   - how multiple augmentations are composed;
   - the exact back-translation model and decoding setup;
   - the projection-head architecture and optimization settings;
   - preprocessing and tokenization details;
   - the exact data splits and sampling procedure.

3. **Ambiguous curriculum definition.** The method is described as linearly increasing augmentation strength, but the actual policy is mostly a stepwise operator-availability schedule. Moreover, the statement that \(L=0\) yields a fixed mixture is mathematically inconsistent with \(c(t)=\min(1,t/L)\), which is undefined for \(L=0\).

4. **Potentially confounded ablation.** The fixed-mixture baseline may not match CurCon in the distribution or number of views generated by each augmentation. Thus, the 0.8-point gain may reflect a different augmentation distribution or training exposure rather than curriculum ordering.

5. **Limited statistical analysis.** Results provide standard deviations over five seeds but no paired significance tests or confidence intervals. Given gains of approximately 0.5–1.5 points, significance should be established.

6. **No comparison to stronger or more directly relevant alternatives.** The paper does not evaluate simpler schedules, random schedules, cosine schedules, continuous augmentation-strength interpolation, or matched augmentation-frequency controls. These are important to isolate the claimed effect.

7. **Questionable generality.** The experiments cover only short English datasets and one encoder. The conclusions should be restricted accordingly.

#### Novelty
The central idea—gradually increasing augmentation difficulty during contrastive training—is plausible but relatively incremental. Curriculum learning and augmentation scheduling are established ideas, and the paper does not offer a substantially new objective, theory, or adaptive scheduling mechanism. The novelty would be stronger if the authors demonstrated that the particular schedule is robust against carefully matched fixed and randomized schedules.

#### Significance
The reported gains are potentially useful, especially in the 100-label regime, but they are modest and may be affected by the asymmetric tuning protocol. The method adds computational overhead and depends on WordNet and machine translation resources. Without stronger controls and broader evaluation, the practical significance is uncertain.

#### Clarity
The paper is generally well structured and readable. The motivation, pipeline, tables, and limitations are clearly presented. However, several methodological descriptions are underspecified or internally inconsistent, particularly the curriculum equation, the \(L=0\) case, augmentation sampling, and the fairness of baseline tuning.

## Final recommendation: **Reject**

The paper presents a clear and potentially useful idea, but the current evaluation does not convincingly establish that the curriculum schedule—not unequal tuning, augmentation-distribution differences, or other confounds—causes the reported improvements. A revision should include matched hyperparameter tuning, carefully controlled schedule ablations, complete implementation details, statistical testing, and stronger comparisons against alternative curricula.
