## Review

### Summary

The paper proposes CurCon, a contrastive intermediate-training method that gradually increases augmentation difficulty during training. The idea is evaluated on four low-resource text-classification datasets with 500 labelled examples per task. CurCon reportedly outperforms fine-tuning, UDA, SimCSE, and CERT, with an average gain of 1.1 points over CERT.

### Strengths

- The problem is practically relevant: exploiting unlabelled data when only a few hundred labelled examples are available.
- The method is simple and easy to integrate into an existing CERT-style pipeline.
- The paper includes multiple baselines, ablations, and results for different label budgets.
- The reported tables are internally arithmetically consistent.
- The paper is generally well organized and clearly written.
- The curriculum idea is intuitively motivated and the reversed-curriculum ablation is potentially informative.

### Concerns

#### Soundness

The experimental evidence is not yet sufficient to establish that the curriculum itself is responsible for the gains.

1. **Unequal hyperparameter treatment.** CurCon is tuned using a 48-configuration grid on each validation set, whereas baselines use hyperparameters reported in their original papers. This creates a potentially substantial comparison advantage for CurCon. All baselines should receive comparable tuning budgets, especially under the same data and compute constraints.

2. **Insufficient statistical analysis.** Results are averaged over only five seeds, and no confidence intervals or paired significance tests are reported. The improvements over CERT—particularly 0.5 points at 1,000 labels—may not be statistically meaningful.

3. **Incomplete implementation details.** Important details are underspecified, including:
   - the exact InfoNCE formulation and projection-head dimensions;
   - sequence truncation and handling of deleted tokens;
   - synonym-selection and WordNet disambiguation;
   - the back-translation model and decoding settings;
   - the exact sampling distribution as the curriculum progresses;
   - whether augmentations are independently sampled for the two views;
   - the number and composition of unlabelled examples used.

4. **Ambiguity in the schedule.** The text says that operators become “available” at thresholds and are then sampled uniformly. This is not clearly equivalent to a smoothly increasing augmentation strength. The role of \(c(t)\) in determining probabilities is not formally specified beyond threshold availability.

5. **Potential issue at \(L=0\).** The formula \(c(t)=\min(1,t/L)\) is undefined when \(L=0\), even though the ablation defines \(L=0\) as a valid fixed-mixture baseline. This can easily be fixed, but the implementation should explicitly define this case.

6. **Ablation limitations.** The comparison between a curriculum and a fixed mixture may confound ordering with the distribution of augmentations encountered during training. A stronger control would match the total number of examples generated by each operator and compare multiple schedules with identical operator frequencies.

7. **Possible compute confounds.** CurCon takes approximately 12% longer than CERT, but the evaluation does not control for wall-clock time, number of update steps, or total augmentation computation. The gains may partly reflect additional computation or different data exposure.

8. **Limited scope.** All datasets are English and relatively short, and only BERT-base is evaluated. This makes the generality of the conclusion uncertain.

#### Novelty

The central idea—progressively increasing augmentation difficulty in contrastive training—is plausible but relatively incremental. Curriculum learning and augmentation scheduling are established ideas, and the contribution primarily consists of applying them to CERT-style text contrastive pretraining with a hand-designed schedule. The novelty is therefore moderate rather than substantial.

The paper would be more novel if it introduced an adaptive schedule, a theoretically motivated difficulty measure, or a more principled method for matching semantic invariance to task requirements.

#### Significance

The reported gains are potentially useful in low-resource settings, especially the 1.6-point improvement with 100 labels. However, the absolute improvements are modest, and the strongest comparison is not fully controlled because of unequal tuning procedures. The practical significance is therefore promising but not yet convincing.

The paper also does not evaluate robustness across more domains, languages, encoder sizes, or substantially lower-resource regimes. Such experiments would be important for supporting the broader claims.

#### Clarity

The paper is clearly structured and readable. The motivation, pipeline, and high-level results are easy to follow. The tables are concise and useful.

Clarity is reduced by several ambiguities in the method description, particularly the augmentation probability schedule, the definition of the \(L=0\) baseline, and the exact experimental treatment of validation and unlabelled data. These are fixable presentation and reproducibility issues rather than fundamental writing problems.

## Recommendation

**Reject**

The paper presents a clear and potentially useful idea, but the current evaluation does not adequately isolate the effect of the curriculum or establish statistical robustness. A revised version should use equally tuned baselines, report confidence intervals and significance tests, clarify the schedule and implementation, add matched-compute controls, and evaluate more datasets or model settings.
